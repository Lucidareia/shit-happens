# Missing cat — field guide (free-roaming cat, village setting)

Procedural search guide. Sources at the end; confidence labelled where it matters.
Working assumption throughout: an outdoor-access cat that hasn't come home is **close by** —
either holed up inside its familiar range (very often shut inside someone's outbuilding) or
sitting tight just outside it. The whole operation is built on that geometry.

---

## Start here — first 60 minutes

1. **Search your own property completely first.** Indoors too (closets, under beds, behind
   appliances), then every structure on the property: shed, garage, cellar, attic, under the
   porch, inside/under any vehicle. Bring a torch even in daylight.
2. **Put out food and water at home** at the normal feeding spot. Leave the usual door/flap
   accessible if possible.
3. **Walk the immediate ring** — your property line plus the adjacent 4–5 properties' edges.
   Move slowly and quietly, torch low to the ground, checking under things. Call in a normal
   calm voice, then **stand still and listen for 30–60 seconds** before moving on.
4. **Message the closest neighbours** (both sides + across): ask them to **open and check
   their own sheds, garages, barns, and outbuildings themselves**, today. A cat shut in by
   accident is one of the most common resolutions, and it will not come out for a stranger.
5. **Start the search log** (template in §8) with these first sweeps, so tonight's ground is
   on record from the start.

Then work the numbered sections below in order. Everything else can wait until these five
are done.

---

## 1. Search geometry — where a free-roaming cat is actually found

The evidence base: a 2018 study of 1,210 missing-cat cases ([Huang et al., *Animals*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/))
and the Missing Animal Response Network's casework ([MAR lost-cat behaviour](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-cat-behavior/)).

- **Indoor-outdoor cats are recovered at a median of ~300 m from home** (the middle 50% of
  cases fall between ~14 m and ~1.6 km) — [Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)
- **Of recovered cats overall, half were within 50 m of home and three-quarters within
  500 m** — [Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)
- MAR's 2013 case data: **84% of lost outdoor-access cats were located within roughly a
  five-house radius** — [MAR](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-cat-behavior/) /
  [MAR missing-cat study](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/missing-cat-study/)
- **Where they turn up:** 83% outdoors — most commonly in a yard, under vegetation/shrubbery,
  or under a porch or deck; **11% inside someone else's building** — [Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)
  In a village that 11% means outbuildings: this is why the neighbour-canvass ask in §4 is
  specifically "check your own structures."

**Priority bands — work inner to outer, and re-cover the inner band before extending:**

- [ ] **Band A — home + 5-house radius (~50 m).** Highest density of recoveries. Sweep
      repeatedly, at the times in §3.
- [ ] **Band B — ~300 m ring.** The median for outdoor-access cats. Full coverage over the
      first days.
- [ ] **Band C — out to ~500 m.** Extend once A and B are logged as covered. Beyond this,
      coverage runs through canvass, notices, and notifications (§4, §7) more than on foot.

**Behaviour that shapes the search** ([MAR](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-cat-behavior/)):
a cat outside its comfort zone **hides in silence and typically does not answer a familiar
voice**. Search is therefore **visual and physical** — you find the cat with your torch and
your knees, not by expecting a reply. MAR also documents a **threshold effect**: some cats
sit tight for **10–12 days** before breaking cover and moving. This is the single best reason
to run the operation as a paced multi-day mission (§8) with standing food stations (§5):
recoveries keep happening well after the first week.

## 2. Systematic physical search — the sweep

Physical search of the area is the **top-performing method** in the study data — used in 96%
of cases and, along with speaking to neighbours and night sweeps with a torch, the
highest-yield strategy ([Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)).

**Terrain checklist for one full village sweep** (tick per zone in the log):

- [ ] Sheds, barns, garages, workshops — inside, behind, and underneath
- [ ] Hay stores, wood piles, log stores
- [ ] Under porches, decks, caravans, and raised structures; crawlspaces and cellars
- [ ] Parked/stored vehicles: underneath, wheel arches, engine bays, open trailers, machinery
- [ ] Dense hedgerows, brambles, undergrowth — walk the full line, torch under it
- [ ] Ditches, culverts, drainage pipes, and roadside verges — walk them as a coverage line
- [ ] Gardens: under shrubbery, behind compost heaps, greenhouses, cold frames
- [ ] Stacks and clutter: pallets, pipes, building materials, disused equipment

**Method per spot:**

- [ ] Move **slowly and silently**. One person per zone; groups make noise.
- [ ] **Torch always on, held low**, sweeping under and behind things — eyeshine is what
      you're looking for, and it works in daylight shadow too
      ([PawBoost](https://www.pawboost.com/blog/7-effective-search-tips-for-finding-your-lost-cat/)).
- [ ] Get **down to ground level** at each candidate spot; look under, not just around
      ([MAR](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-cat-behavior/) — searching means
      crawling under decks and houses, with the owner's permission on others' property).
- [ ] Call **briefly, in a normal calm voice** — frantic tones don't work. Use positive
      sound cues: treat jar shake, tap of the food bowl, pop of a food tin
      ([PawBoost](https://www.pawboost.com/blog/7-effective-search-tips-for-finding-your-lost-cat/)).
- [ ] Then **stop, stand still, and listen 30–60 s**. A faint reply or a rustle is the
      signal; you only hear it if you're not walking.
- [ ] Mark the zone in the log (§8) before leaving it.

## 3. Timing — when a sweep is worth the most

- **Dawn and dusk** are peak cat-activity windows
  ([PetRadar](https://www.petradar.org/en/articles/best-time-of-day-to-look-for-lost-cat)).
- **Late night (after ~22:30) and early morning (before ~06:30)** are the quiet hours: a
  hiding cat is most likely to show itself and to hear you, and you're most likely to hear
  it ([PawBoost](https://www.pawboost.com/blog/7-effective-search-tips-for-finding-your-lost-cat/)).
  Night sweeps with a torch are among the study's top-yield methods
  ([Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)).
- Daytime hours are for the **canvass, notices, notifications, and setup work** (§4, §5,
  §7) — save the on-foot sweeps for the high-value windows.

**Daily rhythm:** dawn sweep → daytime admin/canvass → dusk sweep → one late-night sweep.
Three short sweeps at the right hours beat one long one at the wrong hour.

## 4. Neighbour and village canvass

Speaking with neighbours is one of the top three methods in the study data
([Huang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789300/)), and 11% of cats are
found inside someone else's building — in a village, that's the outbuildings.

**The one critical ask — word it exactly:**

- [ ] "Please **open and physically check your own shed / garage / barn / outbuildings /
      greenhouse** — today if you can, and again any building you next open after having had
      it shut a while." They must check themselves: the cat knows their property, not their
      voice, and won't come out on request.
- [ ] Secondary asks: check under their porch/deck and their parked vehicles; keep an eye
      out at their own dawn/dusk feeding times; call you directly on any sighting —
      **date, time, exact place, direction of travel**, no need to approach the cat.

**Coverage plan:**

- [ ] Door-to-door across Band A/B first (the ~5-house radius, then the ~300 m ring). Log
      each household: spoken-to / leaflet-left / outbuildings-confirmed-checked.
- [ ] **Leaflet/notice**: clear photo, cat's name, your phone number, "please check your
      outbuildings", date last seen. Letterbox any household not home; posters at the spots
      the village actually looks at (shop, bus stop, notice board, post boxes).
- [ ] **Village group chat / community boards**: same photo + ask. One post, then a short
      update every few days to keep it current — updates re-surface the post.
- [ ] Ask the people who cover ground daily: postal carrier, delivery drivers, dog walkers,
      farmers moving between fields.

## 5. Attraction and monitoring setup

The core loop: **feeding station → camera confirms visits → then act** (intercept at the
visit time, or trap per §6). Confirmation before action is the discipline that saves nights.

**Feeding station:**

- [ ] Place food + water in a quiet, sheltered spot at home (porch, shed doorway). A plastic
      storage box on its side keeps rain off ([Humane World](https://www.humaneworld.org/en/resources/how-find-lost-cat)).
- [ ] Use strong-smelling food: cheap wet food, tuna, sardines
      ([3 Retrievers](https://www.3retrievers.com/humane-trap-guidelines.html)).
- [ ] Refresh on a schedule; note in the log what was taken and when. Other animals will
      also eat — that's what the camera is for.
- [ ] A second station is justified only at a location with a **confirmed sighting**.

**Camera:**

- [ ] A trail/wildlife camera on the station is the standard confirm tool; they are cheap,
      effective for exactly this use, and tell you **whether** the cat is visiting and
      **when** ([MAR surveillance](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/surveillance-cameras/),
      [Lost Pet Research](https://lostpetresearch.com/lost-pet-resources/surveillance/)).
- [ ] **Mount low** — standard hunting height (60–90 cm) is too high for a cat; aim at
      ground level at the food ([Lost Pet Research](https://lostpetresearch.com/lost-pet-resources/surveillance/)).
- [ ] No trail cam: an old phone on a power bank with a motion-detect app, filming the
      station through a window, does the job for a home station.
- [ ] Review footage each morning; log every visit with a timestamp. Visit times set your
      intercept/trap schedule.

**Scent-based methods — the honest read:**

- **Litter box / scattered litter outside: not supported.** There is no research showing it
  attracts a lost cat; specialist sources class it as myth, note that reported "successes"
  are cats that came back while a box happened to be out, and point out the scent can draw
  other neighbourhood cats to your station, which works against the setup
  ([Lost Pet Research](https://lostpetresearch.com/2018/10/will-cat-litter-attract-a-lost-cat/),
  [MAR kitty-litter myth](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-pet-behaviors/kitty-litter-myth/)).
  MAR's blunt version: don't scent-lure with litter or feces — it's also a **passive**
  method, and passive methods displace the active ones that actually recover cats.
- **Familiar-scent items at the station or lining a trap** (your worn shirt, the cat's
  bedding): harmless, cheap, and commonly recommended — e.g. 3 Retrievers suggests lining
  the trap floor with your worn shirt
  ([3 Retrievers](https://www.3retrievers.com/humane-trap-guidelines.html)). **Low-confidence,
  low-cost: fine to use, never a substitute for sweeps, canvass, and camera.**

## 6. Humane trapping

**When it becomes the right move:** once the camera confirms the cat visiting a station but
it won't approach — a common pattern, since displaced cats often won't come to people, even
people they know ([MAR trapping](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/trapping-lost-pets/)).
Until you have confirmed visits, keep to station + camera — MAR notes camera-plus-feeding
is often the better play than trapping blind
([MAR surveillance](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/surveillance-cameras/)).

**Protocol** ([3 Retrievers humane-trap guidelines](https://www.3retrievers.com/humane-trap-guidelines.html),
[MAR trapping](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/trapping-lost-pets/)):

- [ ] Borrow a humane box trap (Tru-Catch / Tomahawk type) — vets, rescues, and municipal
      animal services commonly lend them (§7).
- [ ] Site it **where the camera shows the visits**, in shade/shelter, camouflaged with
      surrounding foliage so it blends in.
- [ ] Bait: smelliest food available — most at the far end past the trigger plate, a little
      just inside the door and mid-trap; a smear on nearby branches broadcasts the scent.
      Line the floor with your worn shirt or the cat's bedding.
- [ ] Set it around the camera-confirmed visit window.
- [ ] **Checking discipline: every ~3 hours, never more than 4** — and hourly when it's
      below ~0 °C or above ~27 °C. If you can't meet the check schedule (sleep, absence),
      **close the trap** and re-open when you can. Counter-intuitive but sourced: *not*
      hovering nearby is right for most nervous cats — check on schedule, don't stake it out.
- [ ] Any other animal in the trap: release it where caught, re-bait, re-set. If something
      keeps raiding the bait, the camera identifies the culprit and the setup gets adjusted.
- [ ] Trap-shy cat after several confirmed no-go visits: escalate to a **drop trap**
      (manually triggered, requires you present and watching) or the confinement variants
      ("house trap" — prop the shed/porch door with food inside and drop it on confirmed
      entry) ([MAR trapping](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/trapping-lost-pets/)).
- [ ] Log every trap session: set time, checks, result.

## 7. Registration and notification

Do this on day 1 — it runs in the background for free while you sweep.

- [ ] **Microchip registry**: confirm the registration is active and the phone number
      current; flag the cat as missing with the registry.
- [ ] **Local vets** (yours + every practice within reach of Band C and the neighbouring
      villages): report the cat missing; give description, chip number, your number. Ask
      them to note it for walk-ins and scans.
- [ ] **Municipal animal service / local authority animal department**: file a missing-pet
      report with the same details.
- [ ] **Rescue and rehoming groups** covering the area: same report; ask them to check
      intake against your description and to lend a trap (§6).
- [ ] **Standard info pack** for every notification: recent photo, description (colour,
      coat, sex, collar y/n, distinguishing marks), chip number, last-seen date + location,
      your phone number.
- [ ] Log each notification (who, when, reference number if any) so renewals are easy.

## 8. Running it as a mission

The point of this section: **no in-the-moment decisions.** The plan decides; you execute.

**Search log — one line per action, kept from hour one:**

```
| # | Date | Time | Area/zone | Who | Method (sweep/canvass/station/trap/notify) | Result | Next |
```

- Pre-divide the map into named zones (A1, A2… B1…) matching the bands in §1. A zone is
  either **logged-covered** or **open** — never "probably done."
- Sightings get their own log line with date/time/place/direction; a confirmed sighting
  re-centres the bands on that point.
- The log is what prevents both failure modes: re-covering ground blindly and leaving a
  gap nobody owns.

**Shifts and pacing:**

- [ ] Anchor the day on the three high-value windows (§3): dawn sweep, dusk sweep, one
      late-night sweep. **Time-box each to 45–60 min.** When the box ends, log it and stop.
- [ ] Daytime block (time-boxed too): canvass progress, notices, notification renewals,
      camera review, station refresh.
- [ ] If others help, assign **zones, not vibes** — one person per zone, results into the
      one log. Helpers are ideal for daytime canvass and leaflet drops; keep the quiet-hour
      sweeps to one or two people moving silently.
- [ ] Plan in **multi-day terms from the start.** The evidence says recoveries keep landing
      well past the first days, and some cats hold position 10–12 days before moving
      ([MAR](https://www.missinganimalresponse.com/lost-cat-behavior/)) — the stations,
      camera, canvass, and notifications keep working around the clock between sweeps.
      A sustainable three-sweeps-a-day rhythm outperforms one exhausted all-nighter.
- [ ] **End-of-day review (5 min):** log complete → tomorrow's zones picked → station
      refreshed → camera card checked → done. Nothing else to decide until the dawn sweep.
